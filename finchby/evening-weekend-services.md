# Finchby Paper 4: Common Services a Solo Founder Can Produce on Evenings and Weekends, and Which Ones Compound

*Working draft, 2026-10-04. Fourth paper in the series. Replaces the "services to avoid" lists in the earlier papers.*

---

## 0. Two corrections that reshape the analysis

**Crowded markets are validated markets.** The earlier papers treated bookkeeping, websites, social media, and IT support as categories to avoid. That was too absolute. A crowded category means demand is proven, buyers already understand what they are buying, the sales conversation is short, and incumbents are often slow, opaque, and unresponsive. A common product delivered with excellent service is a legitimate strategy, and it is especially legitimate for a solo founder who cannot afford to educate a market. The categories are re-scored below on that basis.

**Linear skills are out; compounding skills are in.** Recruiting is removed as a direction. Every placement costs the same hours as the last one, and nothing is left behind except a fee. The new scoring column, **Compounding**, asks two questions of every product: does the next unit cost fewer hours than the last, and does the work leave behind an asset (a template, an automation, a Claude skill, a code tool, a content library, an audience) that makes future work cheaper or sellable on its own?

| Linear work | Compounding work |
|---|---|
| Hours in, dollars out, every time | Hours fall per unit as templates, automations, and skills mature |
| Nothing reusable left behind | Every delivery adds to a library you own |
| Revenue capped by your calendar | Revenue can grow while hours stay flat |
| Examples: recruiting, hourly support, one-off calls | Examples: automation building, templated care plans, writing libraries, software, audiences |

## 1. Scoring rules

Each product below is scored on:

- **Demand proof.** Survey or market evidence that buyers already pay for this.
- **Price norm.** What the market charges now, from the sources at the end.
- **Weekend producible.** How much of the delivery can happen asynchronously on weekends and evenings.
- **Service gap.** How badly incumbents serve customers, which is the room to win on service.
- **Compounding** (1–5). Per the definition above.
- **Claude leverage** (1–5). How much of the delivery Claude can carry.
- **Time to first paid client.** Learning plus setup, assuming 10 hours a week.
- **Liability.** What goes wrong if you get it wrong.

## 3. The two-track model

A solo founder with 10 weekend hours and a compounding goal needs two tracks running at once:

- **Track 1, cash flow:** one common recurring service, won on service quality, that gets to the first paying client fast.
- **Track 2, assets:** the compounding skill built out of Track 1's work. Every Track 1 deliverable must leave behind a template, a Claude skill, or an automation. Hours per client per month must fall every quarter, and that number is tracked.

### 3A. Three candidate cores for Track 1

| Core | Monthly price per client | Hours per client per month after templating | Clients for $6K per month | Compounding | Time to first client | Biggest risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Local Business Care Plan** (Google profile and reviews, monthly newsletter, website care, scheduled social) | $500–$900 bundled | 2–4 | 8–12 | 4 | 1–2 months | Low-end software at $30 per month undercuts the tasks; you must sell the service, not the tasks |
| **B. Bookkeeping plus monthly business brief** | $400–$750 | 3–5 | 9–15 | 3 | 3–6 months | Accuracy, year-end crunch, and it was not on your interest list |
| **C. Automation and AI workflow retainers** (CRM, Zapier or Make, Claude skills) | $500–$1,500 plus $1,000–$3,500 setup | 2–4 | 5–10 | 5 | 2–4 months | Buyer skepticism: a third of owners distrust AI accuracy and a third cite data security; sells best to clients who already trust you |

### 3B. Recommendation

**Lead with A, build C from day one, treat B as the fallback if you want maximum demand certainty.**

- **A is the fastest on-ramp** and the most weekend-compatible. Every task is asynchronous, the buyer understands it in one sentence, and the incumbents' failures (no replies, no reports, no explanations) are exactly what good service fixes. Victory can be the first paid client: profile and review management, a monthly partner newsletter to the 1,000 contacts, remnant listings, and website care, at $500–$700 per month. That is a concrete proposal you can make this month.
- **C is the destination skill.** Every care-plan client is also an automation lab. The review-reply skill, the newsletter skill, the posting automation, and the monthly-report generator are built once and reused across every client. After ten clients the same automations become setup projects sold to businesses that do not want the care plan, then skill packs, then possibly a micro-tool. This is the exponential path, and it only works if A is feeding it real client problems.
- **B is the most proven** evening-and-weekend solo business in existence, with a structural shortage behind it, and a bookkeeper sits in the highest-trust seat for selling automation later. It compounds less, takes longer to learn, and did not appear on your interest list. It is here because the evidence is too strong to leave out.

### 3C. Why not the research work that started this

Contact research and scoring, the Victory work, remains a component. It compounds moderately (research skills and prompts are reusable) but AI is commoditizing it quickly, and it is project revenue rather than recurring. It belongs inside the care plan as the partner-newsletter list, and inside automation projects as data cleanup, not as the lead product.

## 4. Winning on service: the operating promises

"Good customer service" is only a strategy if it is specific. These are the promises incumbents break, and they cost discipline rather than money:

1. **Reply within one business day, always.** Weekend delivery is fine; silence is not. Scheduled sends make weekend work arrive on Monday morning.
2. **Fixed prices on a public page.** No hourly surprises, no quotes that take a week.
3. **A one-page monthly report in plain English**, delivered the first weekend of every month: what was done, what changed, what is next.
4. **Onboarding complete in seven days**, with a checklist the client can see.
5. **One quarterly 20-minute call**, on a weekday evening or lunch break, booked by you.
6. **Month to month, cancel any time, and the first month refundable.**
7. **Proactive notices.** "Your site updated; nothing to do" is worth more than it costs.
8. **One owner, one contact.** No handoffs, no ticket numbers.
9. **Everything in a shared folder** the client can open at any time.

Make these the first page of the proposal. They are the product.

## 5. How compounding actually happens

The exponential curve is not a metaphor; it is hours per client falling while price holds.

| Month | Hours per care-plan client | Assets that made the drop |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6 | None yet; everything is manual |
| 3 | 4 | Review-reply skill, post templates, newsletter skill |
| 6 | 3 | Posting automation, report generator, onboarding checklist |
| 12 | 1.5–2 | Client-specific skills, monitoring automation, a pricing page that pre-sells |

Three disciplines keep the curve bending:

- **An asset ledger.** Every month, write down each template, skill, and automation created. If the list is empty, the month was linear.
- **The rule of ten.** Once the same automation has been used for ten clients, package it as a setup project and a skill pack.
- **The audience email.** One monthly email to prospects about what you are building for local businesses. It compounds the sales side, which is otherwise the most linear part of the business.

## 6. Path to $90K–$120K

Gross revenue before tools and taxes. Assumes roughly one new client a month, 20–30% annual churn, and hours per client falling as in section 5.

| Stage | When | Mix | Monthly gross | Hours per week |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First dollar | Months 0–3 | Victory on a care plan; 2 pilot clients at an introductory price | $800–$1,800 | 10 |
| Proof | Months 3–12 | 6–8 care-plan clients, 1 setup project per quarter, first automations built | $3,500–$6,000 | 10 |
| Compounding | Year 2 | 10–12 care-plan clients, 3 automation retainers, 1 project per month, first skill pack | $7,000–$10,000 | 10–13 |
| Choice point | Year 3 | Either stay part-time at $8K–$12K per month, or go full-time and add the writing lines and products | $8,000–$20,000 | 10, or 35–40 |

Unlike the earlier papers, this model can cross the target before you leave the day job, because hours per client fall instead of holding flat. It depends on two numbers, tracked monthly: new clients per month, and hours per client.

## 8. Open questions

1. **Websites.** Are you willing to learn a site builder, or have Claude Code build simple sites? Without that, the care plan loses its entry product and its stickiest line.
2. **Bookkeeping.** Is it a no, or a "not first"? That decides whether core B stays in the plan.
3. **Geography.** Austin-area local businesses first, where the Victory story helps, or remote from the start?
4. **Victory.** Can the first paid proposal be the care plan at $500–$700 per month this month?
